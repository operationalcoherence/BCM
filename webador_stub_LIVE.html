How to use the Bear Canyon Method

THE METHOD
  1. 01Write the goal on the first page of the method's workbook. Provide as much detail as possible. For goals that involve a location, a map will be used later. Goals not centered on a location are able to make use of a lightly structured mapping space.
  2. 02Address the questions as their answers become evident; their order is not as important as the insight they lead you to.
  3. 03One and four are the leading questions. The remainder are guidance for their answer. Include as much information as possible for the purpose of the method and the detail of the visualizations and suggested next steps provided later.
  4. 04Map the context using the answers to the seven questions and additional notes corresponding to the supplied legend, either in this space or a larger one to capture the complexity of the task.
  5. 05Create notes in either map format using text description, emotion, the source the note came from and the voice, as well as the goal and other notes it links to.
  6. 06Work through the method alone first, then with others as needed to enrich the process and its outputs. Involve opposing and supportive voices.
  7. 07Keep the workbook and return to it as things change while working in the context.
  8. 08This method can be iteratively applied in stages until the whole goal is accomplished, a phase of it, or as much of it as reasonably can be.
IN THIS WORKBOOK
  1. 09When writing notes, include as much detail as you are able to: mark the emotion it corresponds to, where it originated (something you saw, were told, a document, or an assumption), and whose voice it came from.
  2. 10In Mapping Change, turn on the emotional landscape to see how far each emotion toward the change reaches.
  3. 11Link a note to an answer, the goal, or another note as supporting, opposing, or reinforcing it. Your links will be shown independently, and part of the larger pattern your answers and notes form, shown in Mapping Synthesis.
  4. 12Navigate to Pattern Analysis and Pattern Synthesis to see how your answers and notes interact. Where they are not explicitly defined by you, patterns suggest interactions based on your work in The Bear Canyon Method (BCM). Pattern accuracy depends on how you move through the method. Pattern Analysis and Pattern Synthesis open once you have answered all seven questions, but are supported heavily by notes.
  5. 13Pattern Analysis reveals recurrence in words and phrases used, what stands out, what is pressing, top actions to take, the basis, patterns formed by you, gaps, and patterns among questions. This page in the BCM workbook also provides a visualization of the seven answers and their relationship and proximity to the goal.
  6. 14In Pattern Synthesis, questions and notes are brought together in a visualization illustrating how all the question answers and notes hang together and form patterns relative to the goal, while offering insights into what the information provided means when brought together, and what the visualized patterns might mean for moving forward.
  7. 15To work with others, each person answers alone and saves their own workbook. Then open yours and choose Combine with another workbook: their answers sit under yours, named, and their notes join your maps. Others could include members of your organization, adjoining, or distant ones, and community members or other stakeholders.
THE FIVE PAGES
  • Mapping Context. Write the goal, answer the seven questions, and add a place if the goal has one, otherwise, use the mapping space.
  • Mapping Change. Mark the information you have already gathered or comes to you while working in the BCM on the place map or the line map, one note at a time, providing as much detail as possible.
  • Pattern Analysis. Looking in: what patterns your answers and notes hold when analyzed together.
  • Pattern Synthesis. Looking out: how questions and notes hang together, what their basis is, what intricacies are formed and the insights they yield, and what to pay attention to.
  • Taking Action. Courses of action distinguished by program or strategy suggested by the BCM, and what still needs to be added to the map or discovered.
WORDS USED HERE
  • Answer. Your reply to one of the seven questions, written as Q.1 to Q.7.
  • Note. A line of text with corresponding data points tied to a symbol on a map, represented as N1, N2, and so on, is a note pinned on the place map; numbered notes are on the line map.
  • Thread. A word or phrase that runs through and relates several answers or notes. A dashed ring on a dot means it is not associated with other answers or notes by a thread.
  • Emotion, source, voice. How a note feels, how you know it, and whose view it carries.
  • Link. A connection you draw from a note to an answer, the goal, or another note.
  • Pattern. A unique to you entanglement of answers and notes formed by words and symbols related by BCM. It suggests; you decide.

Mapping Change

MAPPING CHANGE: INSTRUCTION
INSTRUCTION

Use the blank page, several pages, a poster board, or a whiteboard to map out a collaborative change process with and within the community. It should not be a straight line, but a realistic one that crisscrosses, double-backs, and forms a tangled pattern where some conditions, such as those that need to be redesigned, impede change, and other conditions enable it, meaning the areas that need to be designed or reinforced.

Start by revisiting the goal you want to accomplish and use the answers to the questions to create the landscape of notes that change will be manifested across. What is the pathway to change, and who and what will help or hinder you along it? What are community members and others already doing, where do they support the goal, and where do they oppose it? What options do you have to reinforce and enable the support, and engage constructively with the opposition? What needs to be redesigned or designed?

OBJECTIVE

The resulting map should provide the BCM with a drawing of the change landscape. It will be continually revised as experience is gained and new information is discovered and as patterns are reflected upon.

OUTCOME:

Use the answers to the seven questions to map the change landscape, whether it is concerned with accomplishing the whole goal or a phase or piece of it. This technique can be iteratively applied in stages until the whole goal is accomplished, or as much of it as reasonably can be.
